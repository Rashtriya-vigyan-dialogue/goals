DECLARATION ON THE GOALS OF THE RASTRIYA VIGYAN DIALOGUE

Issued on: 7th November, 2026

DEMAND 01

1- Creation of 'GOV-TAM' Examinations

A High-Powered, Union Cabinet-backed commission composed of suitably qualified and experienced personnel recruited through lateral hiring, for the creation of alternative and relevant pipelines enabling Technical Assistants and pro-bono field experts to contribute to governance systems and participate in government policymaking and actions, in conformity with the spirit and fundamental doctrines of the Indian Constitution. Under Articles 309 and 320(3) of the Indian Constitution, recruitment to public services and posts may be regulated by legislation and applicable recruitment rules, while Article 320(3), read with Article 320(4) and the provisons of Article 320(3), permits specified exemptions from mandatory UPSC consultation through constitutionally authorised regulations, subject to applicable safeguards and limitations.

The necessity of such a mechanism is reflected in existing administrative arrangements, including NITI Aayog's engagement of Young Professionals, Consultants and Senior Consultants, as well as the Aspirational Districts Programme, wherein dedicated Aspirational District Fellows and Technical Support Units have been recognised as important instruments for addressing administrative capacity gaps and facilitating evidence-based implementation at district levels. These examples demonstrate the practical utility of integrating specialised external expertise into governmental institutions, while also highlighting the need for a more systematic and accessible recruitment architecture.

Comparative precedents can also be drawn from the United Kingdom's Civil Service Policy Profession and specialist professional pathways, alongside the United States' statutory provision under 5 U.S.C. §3109 for the temporary or intermittent engagement of experts and consultants. Such arrangements demonstrate alternative institutional approaches to incorporating specialised professional knowledge into government without requiring every expert to follow conventional career civil-service pathways.

Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue proposes the creation of a Government Technical Assistance Mission (GOV-TAM) examination, operating independently of conventional UPSC and SSC recruitment pipelines and exclusively for designated technical-advisory posts. Minimum eligibility qualifications should remain accessible, with a three-tier selection process:

  1. A subjective, theory-based multidisciplinary examination accounting for 75% of the assessment, alongside Class XII Board Examination results accounting for the remaining 25% of the credit. The examination shall require English, a Second Language and four additional subjects selected from a consolidated subject bank incorporating disciplines across Sciences (Physics, Chemistry, Biology, Computer Science, etc.), Commerce (Economics, Accountancy, Business Studies, etc.) and Humanities (Legal Studies, Psychology, Political Studies, History, etc.). Mathematics shall remain an optional subject within the consolidated bank. Subject combinations and their relevance shall be considered while determining eligibility and admission to respective government technical-advisory posts.
  2. An applied assessment evaluating problem-solving abilities, subject-specific knowledge and interdisciplinary reasoning.
  3. An institutional and administrative aptitude assessment evaluating relevant constitutional and legal knowledge, participation in appropriate simulations, ethical judgement and policy-oriented decision-making.

The logistical, grievance-redressal and other examination processes must be administered by a specialised, independent board of experts, drawing institutional inspiration from the IISER committee responsible for conducting the IAT, rather than being entrusted to the National Testing Agency (NTA).

The proposed recruitment architecture must be designed in conformity with Articles 14, 16, 309 and 320 of the Indian Constitution, particularly the principles of equality of opportunity, non-arbitrariness, transparent public recruitment and the constitutional role of Public Service Commissions. The Supreme Court's observations in Secretary, State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2006) concerning constitutional compliance in public employment, alongside its jurisprudence on fairness and transparency in recruitment, should inform the formulation of the proposed mechanism. GOV-TAM must operate through legally permissible recruitment rules and constitutional consultation or exemption arrangements, rather than attempting to circumvent mandatory constitutional requirements.

Furthermore, the proposed framework should examine the relevance of the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments, particularly the institutional importance of Panchayati Raj Institutions, Municipalities and District Planning Committees under Article 243ZD, to ensure that technical expertise is not restricted to Union-level policymaking but can also address local administrative and constituency-level requirements.

The applicable provisions of the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024, as amended in 2026, alongside appropriate non-binding educational frameworks and mechanisms under the National Education Policy, 2020, including but not limited to the National Credit Framework, Academic Bank of Credits and Indian Knowledge Systems-related credits, should also be considered for application to this examination.

The opinions and recommendations of other relevant bodies, including the Central Advisory Board of Education, Ministry of Education and the Nandan Nilekani-led High-Powered Task Force, particularly concerning examination integrity and administration, should also be considered by the proposed Cabinet-backed commission.

For legal and institutional credibility, a dedicated Act of Parliament should be enacted to establish GOV-TAM, define its jurisdiction and regulate its recruitment and advisory functions, in conformity with applicable constitutional provisions.

We call for cross-party consensus on this matter.

DEMAND 02

2- Restructuring of Reservation in Educational Institutions

Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue proposes the restructuring of the existing reservation framework exclusively within educational institutions, through the establishment of an economically prioritised admission system that seeks to balance socioeconomic equity with academic merit.

We propose the introduction of a 60% EWS reservation in educational admissions, with the remaining 40% of seats allocated strictly on merit, across applicable government-funded and other educational institutions, subject to the requisite constitutional and legislative provisions. The proposed framework shall seek to replace existing caste and community-based reservation classifications in educational admissions with a uniform economic-disadvantage-oriented system, while ensuring that the transition is undertaken through constitutionally valid procedures and appropriate safeguards.

The Supreme Court's landmark seven-judge decision in State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024), delivered by a 6:1 majority, permitted the sub-classification of Scheduled Castes for affirmative-action purposes, provided such differentiation is supported by quantifiable empirical evidence demonstrating disparities in relative backwardness and access to benefits. The judgment reinforces the principle that affirmative-action mechanisms must be responsive to the actual circumstances of their intended beneficiaries.

Comparable approaches to socioeconomic prioritisation in educational access can be observed in other developing economies. Brazil's Federal University Quota Law (Law No. 12,711/2012), subsequently revised through Law No. 14,723/2023, reserves at least 50% of undergraduate admissions in federal institutions for students educated in public schools, with half of these reserved places earmarked for applicants from families earning no more than one minimum wage per capita. Indonesia's KIP Kuliah programme provides another relevant example, extending financial assistance to economically disadvantaged students pursuing higher education. Although these mechanisms do not replicate RVD's proposed 60:40 admission structure, they demonstrate the institutional feasibility of incorporating objectively verifiable economic disadvantage into educational access policies.

The proposed restructuring shall be grounded in Articles 14 and 15 of the Indian Constitution, particularly Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 15(6), which provide the constitutional framework for special provisions in educational advancement and admissions. The 103rd Constitutional Amendment and the Supreme Court's judgment in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022), which upheld the constitutional validity of EWS-based reservation, establish an existing constitutional basis for economic disadvantage as a consideration in affirmative-action policy.

Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue further proposes that the existing EWS eligibility framework be comprehensively revised to establish a uniform, transparent and periodically updated socioeconomic assessment mechanism. Such assessment shall incorporate household income, asset ownership, regional economic disparities and other objectively verifiable indicators, ensuring that the benefits of educational reservation reach applicants facing genuine economic disadvantage.

A dedicated legislative framework should be introduced to regulate the proposed admission structure, establish eligibility and verification procedures, prescribe institutional obligations and provide mechanisms for grievance redressal. The framework should also provide for periodic independent audits to prevent fraudulent claims, arbitrary exclusions and the disproportionate concentration of benefits among economically secure sections.

We call for cross-party consensus towards the establishment of this economically prioritised educational admission framework, accompanied by the necessary constitutional amendments and institutional safeguards.

DEMAND 03

3- Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue's support for Higher Education reforms and VBSA bill, 2025 currently being assessed in the respective Standing Parliamentary Committeee

Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue proposes the establishment of a Graduate Contribution and Institutional Reinvestment Scheme (GCIRS), intended to facilitate the development of financially sustainable private higher educational institutions through corporate partnerships, philanthropic endowments, research commercialisation, alumni contributions and Income Sharing Agreements (ISAs). The proposed mechanism seeks to diversify institutional revenue sources, reduce excessive dependence on recurring government grants and conventional student loans, and establish a system in which institutions, corporate stakeholders and graduates share responsibility for sustaining higher education.

Under GCIRS, participating private higher educational institutions shall establish dedicated Educational Reinvestment Funds (ERFs), financed through corporate contributions, institutional revenues, research and intellectual-property commercialisation, philanthropic donations and voluntary graduate contributions. These funds shall finance scholarships, tuition assistance, research infrastructure, laboratory development and institutional expansion. Corporate participation shall be encouraged through industry-sponsored research, endowed academic chairs, laboratory partnerships, research and development agreements and eligible Corporate Social Responsibility expenditure under Section 135 read with Schedule VII of the Companies Act, 2013, which explicitly recognises the promotion of education, special education and employment-enhancing vocational skills as eligible CSR activities. CSR contributions shall remain subject to applicable restrictions and shall not be converted into private investment returns through the ISA mechanism.

A dedicated ISA mechanism shall operate alongside these funds. Participating students may voluntarily enter into agreements through which their tuition fees and other approved educational expenses are financed by the institution or an authorised financing partner. In return, beneficiaries shall contribute a predetermined proportion of their post-qualification income to the ERF, exclusively after crossing a specified minimum income threshold. Each agreement shall prescribe a maximum contribution period, an aggregate repayment ceiling and safeguards for periods of unemployment, financial hardship and other exceptional circumstances. Contributions shall subsequently be reinvested into the education of future cohorts, establishing a revolving institutional financing mechanism rather than an indefinite claim over graduate earnings. Students unwilling or unable to enter such agreements shall retain access to independently funded scholarships and other appropriate financial assistance.

The proposed mechanism draws an ethical and cultural parallel with the traditional Indian principle of Gurudakshina, wherein a student offers a contribution in recognition of the knowledge and guidance received from a teacher. Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue proposes adapting this principle into a voluntary, legally regulated arrangement of intergenerational educational reciprocity, allowing graduates to contribute towards the educational opportunities of subsequent generations without imposing any compulsory religious or financial obligation.

International precedents demonstrate the feasibility of such arrangements. Colombia's Lumni, established in 2002, pioneered the use of private investment funds and Income Sharing Agreements to finance university education, with students contributing a proportion of their post-graduation earnings for a defined period. Its operations extended across Colombia, Mexico, Chile, Peru and the United States, demonstrating the potential of private educational financing in emerging economies. The Australian HECS-HELP framework, although government-backed and structurally distinct from an ISA, provides an additional precedent for income-contingent repayment rather than conventional fixed instalments. These examples should inform the proposed Indian framework without being treated as identical institutional models.

The proposed institutional financing architecture shall draw upon the National Education Policy, 2020, particularly Paragraph 18.14, which encourages philanthropic and public-spirited private higher educational institutions, transparent fee determination, freeships and significant scholarships. The existing Indian institutional framework also demonstrates the feasibility of diversified higher-education financing. The Higher Education Financing Agency (HEFA), jointly promoted by the Government of India and Canara Bank, was established to finance infrastructure and capital development in higher educational institutions through loans, with principal repayments linked to institutional internal accruals, including fee receipts and research earnings. Its financing architecture provides an important domestic precedent for linking institutional development to internally generated revenue rather than relying exclusively on direct grant financing.

Furthermore, centrally funded institutions already demonstrate substantial engagement with corporate and external research financing. IIT Madras's Institute of Computer Science and Research (IC&SR) reported 302 sponsored projects during FY 2024–25, with a sanction value exceeding ₹516 crore, alongside 982 consultancy projects with a reported five-year sanction value exceeding ₹1,022 crore. IIT Kanpur's reported FY 2024–25 figures include ₹633.98 crore in sponsored projects and ₹107.38 crore in consultancy projects. These examples demonstrate that institutional revenues, industry partnerships and research commercialisation can operate alongside public funding, providing a foundation for extending similar diversification mechanisms to participating private institutions.

The legal foundations of institutional financial autonomy and its limitations are reflected in the Supreme Court's decisions in T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002), Islamic Academy of Education v. State of Karnataka (2003), P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005) and Modern Dental College and Research Centre v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016). These judgments recognise institutional autonomy in devising reasonable fee structures while permitting regulatory intervention to prevent profiteering, commercialisation and capitation fees. The proposed framework shall accordingly preserve institutional financial autonomy without permitting exploitative financial arrangements or the commercial appropriation of educational opportunities.

Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue further proposes a dedicated statutory and regulatory framework governing GCIRS, prescribing standardised ISA disclosures, income-verification procedures, maximum contribution percentages, repayment-duration limits, aggregate repayment ceilings, independent auditing, data-protection safeguards and grievance redressal. The legal framework shall clarify the contractual classification and enforceability of ISAs under applicable provisions of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, alongside relevant consumer-protection and financial-regulatory requirements. No agreement shall be permitted to impose disproportionate financial obligations, restrict employment mobility or compromise the fundamental rights of participating graduates.

The proposed framework should additionally be considered alongside the institutional reforms contemplated under the Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan Bill, 2025, which proposes a unified higher-education regulatory Commission and three councils responsible for regulation, accreditation and academic standards. The proposed Viksit Bharat Shiksha Viniyaman Parishad should be considered for appropriate regulatory oversight, while the proposed Viksit Bharat Shiksha Gunvatta Parishad may incorporate institutional financial transparency, sustainability, research productivity and student-welfare indicators into accreditation. The Bill's examination by a Joint Parliamentary Committee provides an appropriate legislative opportunity to consider the regulatory treatment of alternative educational financing mechanisms.

The existing University Grants Commission Act, 1956, particularly Sections 2(f) and 12B, establishes the framework for recognition and eligibility for UGC financial assistance. The proposed restructuring shall therefore distinguish between institutions eligible for statutory public assistance and participating private institutions developing diversified revenue models. GCIRS shall not entail the withdrawal of public investment from government universities, fundamental research, strategically important institutions or students requiring financial assistance. Rather, it seeks to reduce the exclusive dependence of participating private institutions on public grants and conventional student debt through accountable, diversified and reinvestment-oriented financing arrangements.

We call for cross-party consensus towards the establishment of GCIRS and a corresponding legislative framework for the responsible integration of corporate-supported institutional financing and graduate-participatory educational reinvestment.

DEMAND 04

04 — IMMEDIATE TREATING OF ROTE-LEARNING AS A NATIONAL SECURITY THREAT

Rashtriya Vigyan Dialogue considers the continued loss of student lives, institutional harassment, academic intimidation, discriminatory treatment and the systemic deterioration of student welfare a national human-security and strategic-capacity emergency. This is not a demand to treat students as security risks; it is a demand that the State recognise the erosion of an entire generation's intellectual potential, psychological security, scientific capacity and trust in public institutions as a matter of national importance. The Supreme Court's proceedings in Amit Kumar v. Union of India brought the scale of the crisis into institutional focus, recording 13,044 student suicides in 2022, including 2,248 cases attributed to examination failure, and directing the constitution of a National Task Force under former Supreme Court judge Justice S. Ravindra Bhat. The Government must publish the Task Force's findings, disclose the institutional and systemic deficiencies identified through its nationwide consultations, and submit a time-bound Action Taken Report to Parliament. The exercise must not become another report that is received, acknowledged and subsequently buried in administrative files. A country cannot credibly describe its young population as its demographic dividend while allowing its educational institutions to become environments in which students are afraid to question, afraid to fail and afraid to seek help.

We therefore demand the establishment, through dedicated legislation, of an independent Education Rights Protection Bureau (ERPB), with national and state-level offices, jurisdiction over schools, colleges, universities, coaching institutions and other recognised educational establishments, and a mandate to protect students against institutional misconduct. The Bureau must be empowered to receive confidential complaints, initiate suo motu inquiries, conduct institutional inspections, requisition relevant records, summon responsible authorities, investigate allegations of harassment and discrimination, monitor compliance with statutory obligations and refer matters for disciplinary, regulatory or criminal action before the competent authorities. Every institution must maintain an independently accessible grievance-redressal mechanism, a designated student-rights officer and a time-bound complaint-resolution procedure, with strict safeguards against retaliation, academic victimisation or intimidation of complainants. Its jurisdiction must complement, rather than duplicate or displace, existing institutions such as the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights, State Commissions, the University Grants Commission and statutory university ombudspersons. The Bureau must also publish anonymised annual reports identifying recurring institutional failures and the corrective measures imposed. The legal foundations for such a framework already exist, although their present coverage and enforcement remain fragmented. Section 17 of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 prohibits physical punishment and mental harassment within the scope of the Act; the UGC's Redressal of Grievances of Students Regulations, 2023 provide a formal grievance-redressal framework in higher education; the UGC's anti-ragging regulations impose institutional duties to prevent and address ragging; and the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 recognises the importance of mental healthcare and access to services. These protections must be strengthened through enforceable, institution-wide standards that address conduct not adequately captured by the existing provisions. Teacher bullying, public humiliation, threats, discriminatory treatment, arbitrary academic punishment, deliberate obstruction of a student's academic progress and retaliation for raising legitimate complaints must be expressly recognised as forms of institutional misconduct. Professional authority cannot become a licence for intimidation. A teacher's responsibility to educate must be accompanied by an enforceable responsibility to preserve the dignity of the person being educated. Equally, the Bureau must protect educators against malicious complaints through fair inquiry, due process and the right to respond; accountability cannot be allowed to become arbitrary punishment in another form.

A national Learning Rationalisation Programme (LRP) must be instituted to conduct periodic, independent audits of school and university curricula, identifying redundant material, excessive repetition, disproportionate memorisation requirements, outdated content and the accumulation of subjects without corresponding educational value. The objective must not be the indiscriminate reduction of academic standards, but the removal of unnecessary cognitive and administrative burdens so that students can develop deeper conceptual understanding, analytical ability, interdisciplinary reasoning, research aptitude and practical competence. The recommendations of the 1993 Committee on Learning Without Burden, also chaired by Professor Yash Pal, must be revisited in conjunction with the 2009 higher-education report. NEP 2020 itself recognises the need to reduce curricular content to core essentials and create greater space for critical thinking, discussion and experiential learning. These principles must now be converted into measurable implementation requirements, rather than remaining aspirational language in policy documents. Every syllabus must be periodically reviewed by subject experts, practising educators, researchers and student representatives, with publicly disclosed reasons for retaining or removing major curricular components.

The programme must also address the Randall Collins–Ronald Dore problem of credentialism and diploma inflation. In The Credential Society (1979), sociologist Randall Collins examined how educational qualifications can become instruments of occupational screening and social stratification rather than reliable measures of actual competence. Ronald Dore's The Diploma Disease: Education, Qualification and Development (1976) similarly examined the distortion of education when the pursuit of certificates and examination success begins to displace the purpose of learning. Their arguments illuminate a self-reinforcing cycle: as qualifications become increasingly necessary for access to employment, more individuals pursue higher credentials; as credentials become widespread, employers and institutions raise formal entry requirements; students are consequently compelled to acquire still more qualifications merely to remain competitive; and education becomes increasingly dominated by examinations, certificates and competitive selection. The value of a qualification is thereby weakened even as the cost of obtaining it rises. This is the cycle RVD seeks to interrupt. A degree must certify learning, not merely the ability to survive an examination system; an examination must assess competence, not determine the worth of an individual. The Government must therefore encourage competency-based recruitment, practical assessments, demonstrable skills, research portfolios, apprenticeships and recognised alternative pathways wherever professionally appropriate, without compromising essential statutory qualifications in regulated professions. Educational institutions must not be permitted to equate the accumulation of certificates with the development of intelligence, nor should the employment market be allowed to convert every ordinary occupation into an escalating competition for increasingly inflated academic credentials.

We further demand the institutionalisation of interactive, experiential and research-oriented learning. Classroom instruction must increasingly incorporate laboratory work, field studies, supervised projects, simulations, collaborative problem-solving, debates, interdisciplinary assignments and opportunities for independent investigation. Teachers must be enabled to function not merely as transmitters of prescribed material but as mentors who guide students towards discovering, questioning and applying knowledge. Students must be encouraged to challenge propositions respectfully, examine competing explanations and learn through experimentation without being penalised for intellectual curiosity. Educational technology, adaptive learning platforms and AI-assisted tutoring may be deployed to provide individualised academic support, but must remain subject to human supervision, data-protection safeguards, accessibility standards and independent evaluation. Technology must reduce the distance between a student and knowledge, not create another system of surveillance, automated ranking or commercial dependence. The Yash Pal Committee's 2009 report called for a transformation of higher education through multidisciplinary learning, institutional autonomy, the integration of teaching and research, and the dismantling of excessive fragmentation between disciplines. Its recommendations must be implemented through a published national roadmap identifying each recommendation, the responsible ministry or regulator, the implementation deadline, the financial requirement and the measurable outcome. The Government formally received the report in June 2009, and subsequent policy documents, including NEP 2020, have adopted several principles consistent with its recommendations. However, the existence of overlapping policy language cannot be treated as proof of comprehensive implementation. The Union Ministry of Education must publish a recommendation-wise implementation audit, identifying what has been implemented, what remains pending, which institutions are responsible and why specific recommendations have not progressed. The objective is not to reproduce an old report ceremonially, but to ensure that recommendations made in the national interest do not remain indefinitely suspended between committees, ministries and regulatory authorities.

Student welfare must also be treated as an institutional responsibility rather than an optional service or a matter to be addressed only after a crisis. Every recognised educational institution must provide access to qualified counsellors and referral pathways to appropriate mental-health professionals, with adequate staffing, confidentiality and protection against discrimination. Student welfare committees must include trained professionals, educators and elected student representatives. Institutions must establish early-support mechanisms for academic distress, bullying, discrimination, social exclusion and other difficulties affecting student participation. Such systems must prioritise voluntary access, confidentiality and human judgement; they must not become intrusive monitoring systems that profile students or punish them for seeking assistance. The Union Government must coordinate the implementation of these measures through the Ministries of Education, Health and Family Welfare, and Women and Child Development, alongside UGC, NCERT, CBSE, NCPCR and their respective state-level counterparts. International practice demonstrates that student welfare can be embedded into the ordinary functioning of educational institutions rather than treated as an afterthought. Finland's pupil-welfare framework integrates educational support with access to school-health services and multidisciplinary welfare professionals. Japan's Basic Act on Suicide Prevention establishes responsibilities for national and local authorities, with school-related prevention and support forming part of the wider framework. India must study such systems and adapt their institutional principles to its own constitutional, linguistic, economic and administrative conditions. We demand a statutory Student Welfare and Institutional Accountability Dashboard, publishing anonymised, aggregate information on grievance resolution, academic workload, availability of counselling, institutional inspections, discrimination complaints, welfare infrastructure and compliance with student-protection standards. Data must be independently audited and used to identify institutional deficiencies, not to expose individual students or generate algorithmic labels.

Finally, we demand that the Union Government establish a National Educational Accountability and Implementation Mission, with annual reporting to Parliament and corresponding state-level reporting to legislative assemblies. The Mission must coordinate the implementation of the Yash Pal recommendations, Learning Rationalisation Programme, Education Rights Protection Bureau, student-participation mechanisms, workload standards and institutional welfare obligations. Each participating institution must be assessed not merely through examination results, placement statistics or rankings, but through the quality of teaching, research opportunities, student participation, grievance resolution, academic accessibility and compliance with student-rights standards. Public disclosure must be accompanied by corrective action, independent inspections and proportionate consequences for repeated institutional violations.
